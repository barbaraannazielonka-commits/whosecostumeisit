Whose Costume Is It?

A national costume competition. A feather headdress. An apology heard around the internet. Today you'll use a real news story to dig into one of the trickiest questions in modern culture: where is the line between honouring a tradition and taking it?

By the end of this lesson

Learning objectives

  1. Define and distinguish cultural appropriation from cultural appreciation, using precise vocabulary from today's glossary.
  2. Analyse a real news story and identify the different perspectives and voices involved in it.
  3. Compare how different media outlets frame the same event, and evaluate their language, tone and source reliability.
  4. Construct and defend a reasoned opinion — in speech and in writing — on a genuinely debatable cultural question.

Miss Universe Canada contestants apologise for wearing indigenous dress

A costume segment meant to celebrate Canadian heritage instead sparked a public reckoning about respect, sacred symbols, and who gets to wear what.

What happened. During the national costume round of the Miss Universe Canada pageant, two contestants stepped onto the runway in outfits inspired by Indigenous culture. One wore a feathered headdress — traditionally reserved for respected Indigenous leaders — along with a red handprint painted across her face, a symbol linked to the Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women (MMIW) movement. The other wore an all-fur "Arctic Beauty" costume with mukluk boots and braided hair, a hairstyle that carries deep cultural meaning for many First Nations people.

Who spoke up. Ashley Callingbull, a Plains Cree woman from the Enoch Cree Nation and the pageant's first-ever Indigenous winner, was co-hosting the event. She paused the show to address what she'd seen, telling the audience directly that times have changed and that better judgement was expected.

"It's 2026, we should know better." — Ashley Callingbull, pageant co-host and former winner

How the contestants responded. Both women apologised publicly. One wrote on social media that she recognised her costume had caused real hurt within the Indigenous community and that she was grateful for what she'd learned from the experience. The other said her intention had been to celebrate Canada's first peoples, not to disrespect them — but she apologised regardless.

How the organisers responded. Miss Universe Canada issued a statement saying any offence was unintentional, then went further: the pageant director publicly took full responsibility for the mistake and announced two concrete changes. First, a detailed costume guide will set clear rules for every future competition. Second, Callingbull herself has been invited to become the official cultural liaison for the costume segment going forward — meaning she'll help review and approve designs before they ever reach the stage.

Why this isn't new. This is not the first time the competition has faced this exact criticism. Back in 2015, a contestant wore a costume based on a totem pole, which the organisation described at the time as a "misunderstanding" connected to her own heritage. Even then, Callingbull had suggested the pageant hire a cultural consultant — an idea that has now, more than a decade later, become reality.

Language focus: apologising in English

Notice how the people in this story chose their words carefully. These phrases are useful whenever you need to take responsibility — or raise a concern — in formal English.

Taking responsibility

  • "I acknowledge that my actions caused hurt."
  • "It was never my intention to offend."
  • "I take full responsibility for the mistake."
  • "I appreciate what I have learned from this."

Raising a concern respectfully

  • "I was concerned when I saw..."
  • "This raises an important question about..."
  • "We should know better than to..."
  • "It's important that we acknowledge..."

A reasoning framework

  • Invitation: Were members of that culture involved, consulted, or did they invite this?
  • Sacredness: Is the item spiritual, ceremonial, or "just" decorative in its original context?
  • Power: Does the borrower belong to a historically dominant group, relative to the culture being borrowed from?
  • Benefit: Who profits, gets credit, or gains status from this — and who doesn't?
